# Swivel Studio Voice & Tone

## Audience and impression
Speak to organizations hiring a designer and creative teams seeking a collaborator. Convey experienced, strategic creativity: someone who gets up to speed quickly, works independently or within a team, and carries ideas through production.

Let specific decisions and details demonstrate competence. Be clear about capabilities without grand claims or unnecessary modesty.

## Voice
Calm and assured, warm and approachable. Professional without being stiff; personable without making the writing about personality.

Have a clear point of view and explain the reasoning. Sound like an experienced collaborator who listens, contributes, and helps move the work forward.

## Framing and credit
Center the client’s need, the design response, and why it makes sense. Explain meaningful choices instead of narrating a list of tasks.

Make Robin’s contribution explicit in a concise role statement or where relevant in the narrative. Credit collaborators specifically; use “we” only for an actual collaboration. Don’t rely on readers to infer who did what.

Use “I” naturally on About and Contact. In case studies, use it when it clarifies responsibility, then return attention to the work. Describe results concretely and only make claims the evidence supports.

## Length and structure
Let the images carry the portfolio. Use a brief introduction and usually one or two sentences per design decision. Add detail only when it helps readers understand something important.

Headings should be clear or offer an intriguing hook. If the heading doesn’t identify the project, the first sentence should. Answer the hook quickly.

## Language and playfulness
Use serial (Oxford) commas throughout website copy. Include a comma before the final “and” or “or” in a list of three or more items, as in “character, purpose, and audience.”

Use em dashes sparingly, with one space on each side — like this.

Avoid anything that sounds or looks like AI slop. Favor natural phrasing and specific details over stock phrases, inflated language, and formulaic sentence patterns.

Favor concrete language, natural phrasing, and specific design reasoning. Include production details when they reveal useful expertise.

Avoid inflated claims, jargon, repeated “I designed” openings, self-deprecation, and unnecessary banter.

Occasional project-specific wit is welcome, especially in headings. Straightforward headings are equally welcome. Never force a pun or make wordplay a pattern readers must expect. Keep body copy straightforward for now. Personal details belong only when they add relevant meaning.

## Examples
*Illustrative patterns, not claims about existing projects.*

- **Contribution:** “My role covered identity design and print production, in collaboration with the client’s copywriter.”
- **Design reasoning:** “A modular layout accommodated changing schedules while keeping key information easy to find.”
- **Practical expertise:** “The identity was developed to reproduce clearly across small labels, and large-format signage.”
- **Relevant wit:** “Cutting trees to grow healthy forests.”
- **Straightforward heading:** “A clear identity for a growing organization.”

Before keeping a sentence, ask: Does it clarify the project, my contribution, or a meaningful choice? If it only describes what the image already shows, cut it.
